\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Data-centric recursive self-improvement (RSI) asks whether a researcher can turn evidence about a model's limitations into more effective training experience. RSIBench-Data studies this question with a fixed researcher improving a separate learner through controlled training and evaluation services~\citep{rsibenchdata}. Its discovery--reliability gap is consequential: a useful intervention may be found, yet subsequent revisions need not improve it. For tool-using agents, this research also requires an experience source that can execute actions, maintain state, and produce outcomes.

We study the setting in which the researcher observes permitted target tasks and interaction traces but cannot reuse the target backend or verifier implementation. A trace reveals only what happened along one interaction. To train on new actions and goals, the researcher must construct a runnable surrogate: which state persists, what each tool changes, what information each actor can obtain, and what constitutes the requested outcome. For example, a model may claim to update an artifact without executing the update, or apply a whole-record operation when only one field was authorized. Generating more conversations with similar vocabulary does not by itself recreate those decisions.

Executable environment synthesis and adaptive curricula provide important foundations~\citep{envscaler,awm,envharness,spade,rods}. The remaining problem is how to organize repeated research from target behavior. Three distinctions matter. First, a summary of a failure is an interpretation, not a measured capability score; the source interaction must remain inspectable. Second, an environment program is a reusable training asset, not a disposable collection of answers. Third, synthetic reward describes performance on the currently constructed tasks, while target evaluation measures whether training transfers. Conflating these signals can produce increasingly trainable but irrelevant experience.

We present \method{}, a feedback-guided method that maintains an evolving library of complete executable task environments. The studied runs start with an empty environment bank and generic interface examples. A persistent Planner uses source-linked trajectory analysis, fixed-development outcomes, versioned online training feedback, and maintained research experience to choose which environments to create, revise, retain, or pause. Separate implementation workers translate those decisions into complete programs that jointly define task sampling, interaction, and verification. Fresh task instances support continuing online RL, whose outcomes inform the next research decision.

The design has two complementary adaptation axes: expand the library when a target mechanism is missing, and change the challenge or allocation of an existing mechanism when the learner's needs change. A stable requirement map links research hypotheses to actual development-task IDs; scores for those groups are computed from recorded outcomes rather than written by a model. Candidate checks screen execution and discoverability without requiring a teacher solution or a pre-training student probe. The Planner can therefore retain a valid environment even when its current reward distribution is uninformative, revise it, or deliberately allocate no training to it.

Our contribution is this organization of environment research: evidence-linked diagnosis, persistent and selectively editable program assets, and a feedback loop that separates target outcomes from synthetic training signal. These components have precedents individually; their value must be tested through controlled comparisons, not inferred from interface names or successful execution. We report initial three-benchmark Qwen3-4B studies. BFCL shows substantial observed improvement, while $\tau^2$ exhibits later regression and ACEBench shows gains alongside fluctuations on a small test set. These trajectories motivate evaluating the entire evolution process, not only its highest-scoring checkpoint.
